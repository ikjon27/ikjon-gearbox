Jumper's knee (patellar tendinopathy): rehab exercises & recovery plan

Overload of the tendon just below the kneecap. Pain is sharp and pin-point at the bottom of the kneecap during jumping, landing and deep squats.

Common in: Volleyball, basketball, football · Body area: Knee · Updated November 2026

Written by: IKJON teamSources: 2 studies and guidelinesLast checked: 9 November 2026How we write health pages →

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

The patellar tendon joins the kneecap to the shin bone. Every jump and landing pulls on it. In jumper's knee the tendon fibres just below the kneecap are overloaded and become thicker and sore.

How it happens

Lots of jumping, landing and sprinting, especially after a sudden increase in training or a new hard surface.

How serious can it be?

Mild

Pain after sport only.

Moderate

Pain during jumping limits performance.

Severe / long-standing

Pain on stairs and in daily life; can take 3–6 months.

How it heals

Tendons get stronger with heavy, slow loading — not rest. Total rest makes them weaker and the pain returns when you jump again. Expect 12 weeks of steady progress.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • QuadricepsThe muscle that pulls on the tendon — slow heavy squats rebuild the whole unit.
  • GlutesShare landing forces so the tendon takes less.
  • CalvesAbsorb landings with springy ankles.
  • HamstringsBalance the knee and help control landings.

Come back stronger

  • Pain up to 3/10 during loading is OK if it settles by the next morning.
  • Add jumping back in steps: 2–3 days a week, not daily.
  • Keep one heavy leg session a week in the season.

Typical signs

  • Pain at the bottom tip of the kneecap
  • Worse with jumping
  • Stiff in the morning, warms up then worsens

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Sudden pop and cannot straighten the knee (tendon rupture)
  • Big swelling

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Isometric pain relief

Goal: Settle tendon pain.

  1. Spanish squat5 × 45 s

    Isometric · Patellar tendon isometric
    1. Loop a strong band behind both knees and anchor it to a post.
    2. Lean back into the band and sit down until shins are vertical.
    3. Hold, keeping the chest tall.

    Tip: A go-to exercise for jumper's knee (patellar tendinopathy).

  2. Wall sit5 × 45 s

    Isometric · Quadriceps isometric
    1. Back against a wall, slide down to a pain-free knee angle.
    2. Keep weight in the heels.
    3. Hold the position, breathe normally.

    Tip: Isometric holds often reduce knee and tendon pain for a while afterwards.

  3. Calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves
    1. Stand on both feet, hold a support.
    2. Rise high onto the balls of the feet.
    3. Lower slowly over 3 s.

    Tip: Move to the edge of a step for more range.

  4. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

Move on when:

  • Single-leg decline squat pain 3/10 or less
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–8

Heavy slow strength

Goal: Build tendon capacity with slow heavy loading.

  1. Squat4 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes · 3 s down, 3 s up
    1. Feet shoulder-width (hold a weight at the chest to progress).
    2. Sit down between the heels to a pain-free depth.
    3. Stand tall, squeezing glutes.

    Tip: 3 s down, 3 s up builds strength in tendons too.

  2. Split squat4 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes, balance · slow
    1. Long stride stance, back heel up.
    2. Lower the back knee straight down towards the floor.
    3. Drive up through the front heel.

    Tip: Front knee may go over the toes; keep it in line with the foot.

  3. Decline single-leg squat3 × 15

    Strength · Patellar tendon loading
    1. Stand on one leg on a 25° slope (decline board or wedge).
    2. Slowly squat to about 60°.
    3. Use the other leg to help come back up if needed.

    Tip: Classic patellar tendinopathy loading exercise.

  4. Single-leg Romanian deadlift3 × 8

    Strength · Hamstrings, glutes, balance
    1. Stand on one leg with a soft knee.
    2. Hinge forward, the free leg going back, back flat.
    3. Return to standing by squeezing the glute.

    Tip: Hold a wall at first. Hips stay square to the floor.

  5. Single-leg calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves, Achilles
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

Move on when:

  • Strength equal both sides
  • Pain 3/10 or less during and next day
Stage 3 · Weeks 8–12

Spring & jump

Goal: Energy storage: hopping and landing.

  1. Pogo hops4 × 20

    Plyometric · Ankle stiffness, tendon spring
    1. Small quick hops on both feet, knees nearly straight.
    2. Stay on the balls of the feet.
    3. Land quietly.

    Tip: Progress to single-leg pogos.

  2. Drop landing3 × 8

    Plyometric · Landing control
    1. Step off a low box (20–30 cm).
    2. Land on both feet softly, hips back, knees over toes.
    3. Hold the landing 2 s.

    Tip: Quiet landings mean good shock absorption.

  3. Lateral bounds3 × 8

    Plyometric · Side-to-side power & control
    1. Jump sideways from one leg to the other.
    2. Land and stick for 2 s before the next.
    3. Increase distance gradually.

    Tip: Knee must not cave inward on landing.

  4. Single-leg hop & stick3 × 6

    Plyometric · Power, landing on one leg
    1. Hop forward on one leg.
    2. Land softly and hold for 3 s.
    3. Progress distance, then add lateral and diagonal hops.

    Tip: Compare distance with the uninjured leg — target 90%+.

  5. Rear-foot elevated split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Single-leg strength
    1. Back foot on a bench behind you.
    2. Lower down on the front leg under control.
    3. Drive up through the front foot.

    Tip: Add dumbbells or a backpack when 3×10 is easy.

Move on when:

  • Repeated jumping without next-day flare
Stage 4 · Week 12+

Sport

Goal: Return to full jumping volume.

  1. Agility & cutting drills4 × 20 s

    Plyometric · Sport-specific movement
    1. Ladder drills, then cone shuttles.
    2. Planned cuts at 45°, later 90°.
    3. Finally unplanned cuts reacting to a partner's signal.

    Tip: Unplanned cutting is the last step before full training.

  2. Single-leg hop & stick3 × 8

    Plyometric · Power, landing on one leg
    1. Hop forward on one leg.
    2. Land softly and hold for 3 s.
    3. Progress distance, then add lateral and diagonal hops.

    Tip: Compare distance with the uninjured leg — target 90%+.

  3. Decline single-leg squat3 × 15

    Strength · Patellar tendon loading · keep 2×/week
    1. Stand on one leg on a 25° slope (decline board or wedge).
    2. Slowly squat to about 60°.
    3. Use the other leg to help come back up if needed.

    Tip: Classic patellar tendinopathy loading exercise.

Move on when:

  • Full training with pain 2/10 or less
  • Keep tendon loading twice weekly

Important: Tendons respond to progressive load, not rest. Total rest makes them weaker.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Moderate

  • 12 weeks of heavy slow resistance training gave good short- and long-term results and the highest patient satisfaction; steroid injections helped short-term but results got worse later.
  • Isometric holds and slow isotonic exercise both reduced in-season tendon pain, with no clear winner — choose what you tolerate.

Sources: Kongsgaard et al. 2009, Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports (RCT); van Ark et al. 2016, Journal of Science and Medicine in Sport (RCT)

Common questions

Should I rest completely with jumper's knee?

No. Complete rest makes the tendon weaker and the pain often returns when you jump again. Reduce jumping, but keep loading the tendon with slow, heavy exercises such as wall sits and slow squats.

How much pain is OK during jumper's knee exercises?

Pain up to about 3–4 out of 10 during the exercise is usually fine if it settles by the next morning. If the knee is more painful the next day, make the next session a little easier.

How long does jumper's knee take to get better?

Tendons adapt slowly. Most people need about 12 weeks of steady strengthening, and long-standing cases can take 3–4 months or more. Improvement is usually gradual rather than sudden.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Sudden pop and cannot straighten the knee (tendon rupture); Big swelling.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
