Meniscus tear: rehab exercises & recovery plan

The menisci are two cartilage cushions in the knee. Tears come from twisting on a bent knee, or slowly with age. Many heal well with exercise alone.

Common in: Football, squatting sports · Body area: Knee · Updated November 2026

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

The meniscus is a C-shaped cushion of cartilage between the thigh bone and the shin bone. Each knee has two — inner (medial) and outer (lateral). They absorb shock and help keep the knee steady.

How it happens

Usually a twist on a bent knee with the foot planted — turning to pass, or rotating while squatting. After about 35, tears can also appear slowly with everyday wear.

How serious can it be?

Small, stable tear

A small split near the edge. The knee still bends and straightens fully. Most settle with exercise in 4–8 weeks.

Larger tear

Swelling after activity, catching or clicking, deep squats hurt. Exercise is still usually the first treatment; some people need a scan.

Locked knee (flap tear)

A torn flap blocks the knee so it can't straighten. See a doctor soon — this often needs keyhole surgery.

How it heals

The outer third of the meniscus has a blood supply and can heal. The inner part heals poorly, but the knee copes very well when the muscles around it are strong. For wear-type tears, 12 weeks of exercise worked as well as surgery.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • QuadricepsYour main shock absorber — strong quads take load off the meniscus.
  • GlutesControl the hip so the knee doesn't twist or cave inward.
  • Gluteus medius (side hip)Keeps the knee in line when you land or squat on one leg.
  • HamstringsProtect the back of the knee and control rotation.
  • CalvesAbsorb force when you run and land.

Come back stronger

  • Increase squat depth slowly — pain should stay at 3/10 or less and settle by the next day.
  • Practise soft landings with the knee over the middle toes.
  • Keep 2 leg-strength sessions a week even after you're back to sport.

Typical signs

  • Pain on the inner or outer joint line
  • Swelling over a day
  • Catching, clicking, or difficulty squatting deep

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Knee locked and cannot straighten fully
  • Repeated giving way

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Settle & move

Goal: Reduce swelling, restore movement.

  1. Quad sets3 × 10

    Isometric · Quadriceps activation · 5 s hold
    1. Sit or lie with the leg straight and a rolled towel under the knee.
    2. Tighten the thigh to press the back of the knee into the towel.
    3. Hold, then relax fully.

    Tip: Watch the kneecap glide up as the muscle switches on.

  2. Straight leg raise3 × 10

    Strength · Quadriceps, hip flexors
    1. Lie on your back, other knee bent.
    2. Lock the straight knee with a quad set first.
    3. Lift the leg to the height of the other knee, lower slowly.

    Tip: If the knee bends during the lift (a 'lag'), go back to quad sets.

  3. Heel slides3 × 15

    Mobility · Knee bending range
    1. Lie on your back.
    2. Slide the heel towards your bottom as far as comfortable.
    3. Slide back to straight.

    Tip: Use a towel around the foot to help if needed.

  4. Stationary bike10 min

    Cardio · Knee range, fitness
    1. Seat high enough that the knee is almost straight at the bottom.
    2. Low resistance.
    3. Steady pedalling.

    Tip: Rock back and forth first if a full turn is not yet possible.

  5. Terminal knee extension3 × 15

    Strength · Vastus medialis, last degrees of extension
    1. Band around the back of the knee, anchored in front.
    2. Start with the knee slightly bent.
    3. Push the knee back to fully straight against the band, squeeze 2 s.

    Tip: Slow and controlled, no snapping into lock.

Move on when:

  • Little swelling
  • Near-full range
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–6

Base strength

Goal: Quad, hip and balance work.

  1. Mini squat (0–45°)3 × 12

    Strength · Quads, glutes
    1. Feet hip-width, hold a support if needed.
    2. Sit back a little as if to a high chair, knees to about 45°.
    3. Push through both heels to stand.

    Tip: Knees track over the 2nd toe, not caving in.

  2. Step-ups3 × 10

    Strength · Quads, glutes
    1. Face a low step (10–20 cm).
    2. Step up with the working leg and stand tall.
    3. Step down slowly with the other leg.

    Tip: Raise the step height as strength improves.

  3. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

  4. Wall sit3 × 30 s

    Isometric · Quadriceps isometric
    1. Back against a wall, slide down to a pain-free knee angle.
    2. Keep weight in the heels.
    3. Hold the position, breathe normally.

    Tip: Isometric holds often reduce knee and tendon pain for a while afterwards.

  5. Single-leg balance3 × 30 s

    Balance · Balance, ankle reflexes
    1. Stand on one leg, knee soft.
    2. Progress: eyes closed → on a pillow → catching a ball.
    3. Stay tall.

    Tip: Balance training reduces repeat ankle sprains.

  6. Side-lying hip abduction3 × 12

    Strength · Glute medius
    1. Lie on your side, bottom knee bent.
    2. Lift the straight top leg slightly behind the body.
    3. Lower slowly, toes pointing forward.

    Tip: Strong hip abductors reduce knee 'caving in'.

Move on when:

  • Walking & stairs without pain
  • Single-leg stand 30 s
Stage 3 · Weeks 6–10

Single-leg control

Goal: Strength through more range.

  1. Split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes, balance
    1. Long stride stance, back heel up.
    2. Lower the back knee straight down towards the floor.
    3. Drive up through the front heel.

    Tip: Front knee may go over the toes; keep it in line with the foot.

  2. Forward step-down3 × 10

    Strength · Eccentric quad control
    1. Stand on a step on the working leg.
    2. Slowly lower the other heel to touch the floor in front.
    3. Push back up without locking the knee hard.

    Tip: Knee stays in line with the foot. Use a mirror.

  3. Single-leg Romanian deadlift3 × 8

    Strength · Hamstrings, glutes, balance
    1. Stand on one leg with a soft knee.
    2. Hinge forward, the free leg going back, back flat.
    3. Return to standing by squeezing the glute.

    Tip: Hold a wall at first. Hips stay square to the floor.

  4. Y-balance reach3 × 5

    Balance · Dynamic balance
    1. Stand on one leg.
    2. Reach the other foot forward, back-left, back-right as far as you can.
    3. Touch lightly, return without falling.

    Tip: Measure reach distance; compare sides.

  5. Single-leg calf raise3 × 12

    Strength · Calves, Achilles
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

Move on when:

  • Squat to 90° pain-free
  • No swelling after training
Stage 4 · Week 10+

Run & pivot

Goal: Back to running and turning.

  1. Walk–jog intervals20 min

    Cardio · Return to running
    1. Start: 1 min jog / 1 min walk × 10.
    2. Each pain-free session, add jog time.
    3. Target 20–30 min continuous easy running.

    Tip: Run on flat, even ground first.

  2. Lateral bounds3 × 6

    Plyometric · Side-to-side power & control
    1. Jump sideways from one leg to the other.
    2. Land and stick for 2 s before the next.
    3. Increase distance gradually.

    Tip: Knee must not cave inward on landing.

  3. Agility & cutting drills4 × 20 s

    Plyometric · Sport-specific movement
    1. Ladder drills, then cone shuttles.
    2. Planned cuts at 45°, later 90°.
    3. Finally unplanned cuts reacting to a partner's signal.

    Tip: Unplanned cutting is the last step before full training.

  4. Single-leg hop & stick3 × 5

    Plyometric · Power, landing on one leg
    1. Hop forward on one leg.
    2. Land softly and hold for 3 s.
    3. Progress distance, then add lateral and diagonal hops.

    Tip: Compare distance with the uninjured leg — target 90%+.

Move on when:

  • Running, cutting and hopping without swelling

Important: After a meniscus REPAIR (stitches), surgeons usually limit deep bending and loaded squats for 6+ weeks. Follow their limits.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Strong

  • For degenerative tears in middle-aged adults, 12 weeks of supervised exercise was as good as keyhole surgery for pain and function at 2 years, and built more thigh strength.
  • Traumatic tears in young athletes, or a knee that locks, may still need a surgical opinion.

Sources: Kise et al. 2016, BMJ (RCT, 140 patients)

Common questions

How long does meniscus tear take to heal?

It depends on severity. Mild: about 30 days (about 4 weeks). Moderate: about 60 days (about 9 weeks). After repair surgery: about 120 days (about 4 months). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Knee locked and cannot straighten fully; Repeated giving way.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
