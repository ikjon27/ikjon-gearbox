Groin (adductor) strain: rehab exercises & recovery plan

Strain of the inner thigh muscles, usually from kicking, changing direction or stretching into a split.

Common in: Football, hockey, tennis · Body area: Hip & groin · Updated November 2026

Written by: IKJON teamSources: 2 studies and guidelinesLast checked: 9 November 2026How we write health pages →

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

Groin pain has 5 main types. Doctors group it by where it comes from (Doha agreement). Pick the one that matches where it hurts:

Adductor-relatedIliopsoas-relatedInguinal-relatedPubic-relatedHip-related

What's injured

The adductors are the inner-thigh muscles that pull your legs together. The adductor longus is the one most often strained, usually close to where it attaches to the pubic bone.

How it happens

Kicking hard, changing direction quickly, or slipping into a split. Also builds up slowly when training load jumps.

How serious can it be?

Grade 1

A few fibres strained — tight and sore when kicking. 1–3 weeks.

Grade 2

Partial tear — pain walking fast, weak squeeze. 3–8 weeks.

Grade 3 / long-standing

Big tear, or groin pain for months. Needs a structured programme; see a sports doctor if not improving.

How it heals

Muscle tissue heals in a few weeks, but the tendon near the bone is slower. Strengthening (not just rest and stretching) is what works — active training beat passive treatment for long-standing groin pain.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • Adductors (inner thigh)The injured muscles — make them strong with squeezes and the Copenhagen plank.
  • Core (abs & obliques)Abs and obliques share the load across the pelvis with the adductors.
  • GlutesBalance the hip so the inner thigh isn't overworked.
  • Hip flexorsOften tight and weak together with the adductors in kicking sports.

Come back stronger

  • Keep the Copenhagen plank in your weekly routine — it cut groin problems by 41% in footballers.
  • Build kicking and sprint volume gradually after time off.
  • Test your squeeze: both sides should feel equally strong.

Typical signs

  • Pain in the inner thigh or groin
  • Pain squeezing the knees together
  • Pain kicking

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Swelling or lump in the groin (possible hernia)
  • Fever, pain urinating
  • Deep hip pain with clicking

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Week 0–1

Isometric

Goal: Pain-free squeezes.

  1. Adductor squeeze5 × 10 s

    Isometric · Groin isometric
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, ball or fist between the knees.
    2. Squeeze at a comfortable effort.
    3. Hold, then relax.

    Tip: Increase squeeze effort as pain allows.

  2. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

  3. Stationary bike10 min

    Cardio · Knee range, fitness
    1. Seat high enough that the knee is almost straight at the bottom.
    2. Low resistance.
    3. Steady pedalling.

    Tip: Rock back and forth first if a full turn is not yet possible.

Move on when:

  • Full squeeze pain 2/10 or less
Stage 2 · Weeks 1–4

Strength

Goal: Adductor and hip strength.

  1. Copenhagen plank3 × 15 s

    Strength · Adductors (groin) · short lever
    1. Side plank with the top leg on a bench.
    2. Short lever: knee on the bench. Long lever: ankle on the bench.
    3. Lift hips and hold, bottom leg free.

    Tip: Shown to cut groin injuries in football by around 40%.

  2. Lateral lunge3 × 8

    Strength · Adductors, glutes
    1. Wide stance.
    2. Shift to one side, bending that knee, other leg straight.
    3. Push back to centre.

    Tip: Keep the bent knee in line with the foot.

  3. Adductor squeeze3 × 15 s

    Isometric · Groin isometric · hard
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, ball or fist between the knees.
    2. Squeeze at a comfortable effort.
    3. Hold, then relax.

    Tip: Increase squeeze effort as pain allows.

  4. Single-leg Romanian deadlift3 × 8

    Strength · Hamstrings, glutes, balance
    1. Stand on one leg with a soft knee.
    2. Hinge forward, the free leg going back, back flat.
    3. Return to standing by squeezing the glute.

    Tip: Hold a wall at first. Hips stay square to the floor.

  5. Hip flexor stretch3 × 30 s

    Stretch · Hip front flexibility
    1. Half-kneel, back knee on a cushion.
    2. Tuck the pelvis under and squeeze the back glute.
    3. Shift forward gently.

    Tip: Breathe slowly; no bouncing.

  6. Side-lying adduction3 × 12

    Strength · Inner thigh (adductors)
    1. Lie on your side, top leg bent in front.
    2. Lift the straight bottom leg up off the floor.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: An early, gentle groin strengthener.

Move on when:

  • Lateral lunge pain-free
  • Short Copenhagen 30 s
Stage 3 · Weeks 4+

Return to sport

Goal: Direction changes and kicking.

  1. Copenhagen plank3 × 20 s

    Strength · Adductors (groin) · long lever
    1. Side plank with the top leg on a bench.
    2. Short lever: knee on the bench. Long lever: ankle on the bench.
    3. Lift hips and hold, bottom leg free.

    Tip: Shown to cut groin injuries in football by around 40%.

  2. Lateral bounds3 × 8

    Plyometric · Side-to-side power & control
    1. Jump sideways from one leg to the other.
    2. Land and stick for 2 s before the next.
    3. Increase distance gradually.

    Tip: Knee must not cave inward on landing.

  3. Agility & cutting drills4 × 20 s

    Plyometric · Sport-specific movement
    1. Ladder drills, then cone shuttles.
    2. Planned cuts at 45°, later 90°.
    3. Finally unplanned cuts reacting to a partner's signal.

    Tip: Unplanned cutting is the last step before full training.

  4. Running strides6 × 1

    Cardio · Speed, hamstring loading
    1. Run 60–80 m, building up gradually.
    2. Reach the target speed in the middle third.
    3. Walk back to recover.

    Tip: Progress 60% → 75% → 90% → 100% effort across sessions.

  5. Single-leg lateral hop3 × 8

    Plyometric · Ankle & knee control
    1. Stand on one leg.
    2. Hop sideways over a line and land softly.
    3. Hop back. Stick each landing.

    Tip: Knee stays over the foot.

Move on when:

  • Kicking long passes pain-free
  • Full cutting

Important: The Copenhagen plank is one of the most effective groin injury preventers — keep it in your routine for good.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Strong

  • A simple Copenhagen adduction programme lowered the risk of groin problems by 41% in football players over a season.
  • For long-standing adductor-related groin pain, an active strengthening programme beat passive physiotherapy: far more athletes returned to sport.

Sources: Harøy et al. 2019, British Journal of Sports Medicine (cluster RCT, 652 players); Hölmich et al. 1999, The Lancet (RCT)

Common questions

How long does a groin strain take to heal?

A mild groin strain often takes 1–3 weeks, a moderate one 4–8 weeks and a severe one 2–3 months. Long kicks and fast changes of direction come back last.

Can I play football with a groin strain?

Not while squeezing your knees together, kicking or sprinting is painful. Playing too early is the most common reason groin pain becomes long-lasting.

How do I know if it is a groin strain or something else?

An adductor strain hurts on the inside of the upper thigh and when you squeeze your knees together. Pain higher up in the lower belly, over the pubic bone or deep in the hip points to other types of groin pain, so see a physiotherapist.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Swelling or lump in the groin (possible hernia); Fever, pain urinating; Deep hip pain with clicking.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
