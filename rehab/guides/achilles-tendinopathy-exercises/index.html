Achilles tendinopathy: rehab exercises & recovery plan

Overload of the Achilles tendon. Pain is 2–6 cm above the heel (mid-portion) or where the tendon meets the heel bone (insertional). Worst in the morning.

Common in: Running, football, tennis · Body area: Ankle · Updated November 2026

Written by: IKJON teamSources: 1 studies and guidelinesLast checked: 9 November 2026How we write health pages →

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

The Achilles tendon joins the calf muscles to the heel bone. It is the strongest tendon in the body. In tendinopathy the tendon is overloaded and thickens — most often 2–6 cm above the heel.

How it happens

A rise in running, jumping or hill training, new shoes, or a hard surface — often in people over 30 when the tendon recovers more slowly.

How serious can it be?

Mild

Stiff in the morning, fine once warm.

Moderate

Pain limits running.

Severe

Pain walking, thickened tendon. Can take 3–6 months.

How it heals

Tendons rebuild with steady, heavy calf loading over about 12 weeks. Rest alone makes them weaker. Pain up to 3–4/10 during loading is acceptable if it settles by the next morning.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • CalvesThe core treatment — heavy calf raises with straight and bent knees.
  • HamstringsHelp push off so the calf and tendon aren't overloaded.
  • GlutesPower for running that spares the lower leg.
  • Foot musclesStable feet reduce twisting load on the tendon.

Come back stronger

  • Keep 2 calf-strength sessions a week forever.
  • Increase running by about 10% a week; add hills last.
  • Morning stiffness is your guide: if it is worse the next day, ease off.

Typical signs

  • Morning stiffness in the tendon
  • Thickening or tender lump
  • Pain at the start of running

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Sudden pop or feeling of being kicked in the heel (rupture)
  • Cannot rise onto the toes

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Isometric relief

Goal: Settle pain while keeping the tendon loaded.

  1. Isometric calf hold5 × 45 s

    Isometric · Achilles isometric
    1. Rise onto the toes halfway on one or two legs.
    2. Hold the position.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Helps settle Achilles pain before heavier loading.

  2. Bent-knee calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Soleus
    1. Stand with knees bent about 30° (or seated with weight on the knees).
    2. Rise onto the toes keeping the knee bend.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: The soleus takes huge load when running.

  3. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

Move on when:

  • Morning stiffness reducing
  • Two-leg calf raise pain 3/10 or less
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–8

Strength

Goal: Progressive slow calf loading.

  1. Calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves · 3 s down
    1. Stand on both feet, hold a support.
    2. Rise high onto the balls of the feet.
    3. Lower slowly over 3 s.

    Tip: Move to the edge of a step for more range.

  2. Single-leg calf raise3 × 12

    Strength · Calves, Achilles · add backpack weight
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

  3. Eccentric heel drop3 × 15

    Strength · Achilles tendon
    1. Stand on a step on the balls of both feet.
    2. Shift onto the injured leg and lower the heel slowly over 3 s.
    3. Use both legs to come back up.

    Tip: Insertional (heel-bone) pain: don't drop below step level.

  4. Bent-knee calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Soleus
    1. Stand with knees bent about 30° (or seated with weight on the knees).
    2. Rise onto the toes keeping the knee bend.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: The soleus takes huge load when running.

Move on when:

  • 25 single-leg calf raises
  • Pain 2/10 or less next morning
Stage 3 · Weeks 8–12

Spring

Goal: Tendon energy storage.

  1. Pogo hops4 × 20

    Plyometric · Ankle stiffness, tendon spring
    1. Small quick hops on both feet, knees nearly straight.
    2. Stay on the balls of the feet.
    3. Land quietly.

    Tip: Progress to single-leg pogos.

  2. Single-leg calf raise4 × 8

    Strength · Calves, Achilles · heavy
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

  3. Walk–jog intervals20 min

    Cardio · Return to running
    1. Start: 1 min jog / 1 min walk × 10.
    2. Each pain-free session, add jog time.
    3. Target 20–30 min continuous easy running.

    Tip: Run on flat, even ground first.

  4. Lateral bounds3 × 6

    Plyometric · Side-to-side power & control
    1. Jump sideways from one leg to the other.
    2. Land and stick for 2 s before the next.
    3. Increase distance gradually.

    Tip: Knee must not cave inward on landing.

  5. Skipping (A-skips)3 × 20

    Cardio · Running coordination, calf spring
    1. Skip forward driving one knee up.
    2. Land on the ball of the foot.
    3. Swing the arms opposite to the legs.

    Tip: Great warm-up before running and sprinting.

Move on when:

  • Single-leg pogos ×20 pain-free
Stage 4 · Week 12+

Sport

Goal: Speed, sprints and sport.

  1. Running strides6 × 1

    Cardio · Speed, hamstring loading
    1. Run 60–80 m, building up gradually.
    2. Reach the target speed in the middle third.
    3. Walk back to recover.

    Tip: Progress 60% → 75% → 90% → 100% effort across sessions.

  2. Single-leg hop & stick3 × 6

    Plyometric · Power, landing on one leg
    1. Hop forward on one leg.
    2. Land softly and hold for 3 s.
    3. Progress distance, then add lateral and diagonal hops.

    Tip: Compare distance with the uninjured leg — target 90%+.

  3. Single-leg calf raise3 × 12

    Strength · Calves, Achilles · keep 2×/week
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

Move on when:

  • Full training, keep calf strength work

Important: Pain up to 3/10 during exercise is acceptable if it settles by the next morning.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Strong

  • Both heavy slow calf training and eccentric heel drops improved Achilles function over 12 weeks, and results lasted to 1 year.
  • People stuck to the heavy slow programme better (92% vs 78% of sessions).

Sources: Beyer et al. 2015, American Journal of Sports Medicine (RCT)

Common questions

Should I stretch my Achilles tendon if it hurts?

Stretching alone rarely fixes Achilles tendon pain and can irritate it. Slow, heavy calf raises are the main treatment because they make the tendon stronger.

Why is my Achilles stiff in the morning?

Morning stiffness that eases after a few minutes of walking is typical of Achilles tendinopathy. If the stiffness lasts longer than before, your recent load was probably too high.

When should I worry about Achilles pain?

Get urgent help if you felt a sudden snap, as if kicked in the back of the heel, and cannot stand on tiptoe. This can be a rupture, which needs prompt treatment.

How long does achilles tendinopathy take to heal?

It depends on severity. Mild: about 60 days (about 9 weeks). Moderate: about 90 days (about 3 months). Severe: about 120 days (about 4 months). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Sudden pop or feeling of being kicked in the heel (rupture); Cannot rise onto the toes.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
