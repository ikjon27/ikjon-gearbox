Rotator cuff / shoulder impingement: rehab exercises & recovery plan

Pain on the side or front of the shoulder when lifting the arm, from irritation of the rotator cuff tendons. Load management and strengthening work for most people.

Common in: Volleyball, swimming, throwing, gym · Body area: Shoulder · Updated November 2026

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

The rotator cuff is four small muscles whose tendons wrap around the ball of the shoulder and keep it centred in the socket. The top tendon (supraspinatus) runs under the bony roof of the shoulder (acromion) and is the one usually irritated.

How it happens

Repeated lifting, throwing or overhead work, or a sudden rise in gym training. Weak shoulder-blade muscles and stiff upper back let the tendon get squeezed and overloaded.

How serious can it be?

Mild

Pain only when throwing or working overhead.

Moderate

Dressing and reaching up hurt; painful arc lifting the arm sideways.

Severe

Pain at night and a weak arm. A sudden loss of strength after a fall needs a doctor (possible tear).

How it heals

Tendon pain improves with progressive strengthening over 6–12 weeks. Exercise gives results similar to surgery for most people with impingement-type pain.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • Rotator cuffThe rotator cuff itself — band external and internal rotation.
  • Upper backShoulder-blade muscles give the cuff a stable base.
  • Shoulders (deltoids)Strong shoulders share the lifting load.
  • Core (abs & obliques)A stable trunk improves throwing and lifting technique.

Come back stronger

  • Work in a pain-free range first, then raise the arm higher week by week.
  • Balance pushing exercises with pulling (rows, Y-raises).
  • Warm up the cuff with a band before throwing or overhead lifting.

Typical signs

  • Pain lifting the arm overhead
  • Pain lying on that side
  • Painful arc between 60° and 120°

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Cannot lift the arm at all after a fall (possible tear or dislocation)
  • Pain with fever or redness
  • Pain spreading down the arm with numbness

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Settle

Goal: Pain relief and pain-free movement.

  1. Pendulum2 × 1 min

    Mobility · Gentle shoulder movement
    1. Lean forward with a hand on a table.
    2. Let the sore arm hang loose.
    3. Make small circles by swaying your body.

    Tip: Arm stays relaxed — the body moves it.

  2. Shoulder blade squeeze3 × 10

    Isometric · Scapular muscles
    1. Sit or stand tall.
    2. Draw shoulder blades back and down.
    3. Hold 5 s.

    Tip: No shrugging up to the ears.

  3. Wall slide3 × 10

    Mobility · Overhead control · pain-free range
    1. Forearms on a wall, elbows at shoulder height.
    2. Slide arms up the wall in a pain-free range.
    3. Slide back down slowly.

    Tip: Stop below the point of pain.

  4. Open book2 × 8

    Mobility · Upper back rotation
    1. Lie on your side, knees bent, arms together in front.
    2. Open the top arm across to the other side, following it with your eyes.
    3. Return slowly.

    Tip: Breathe out as you open.

Move on when:

  • Arm lifts to shoulder height comfortably
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–6

Cuff strength

Goal: Rotator cuff and shoulder blade strength.

  1. Band external rotation3 × 15

    Strength · Rotator cuff
    1. Elbow at your side, towel under it, band in hand.
    2. Rotate the forearm outward.
    3. Return slowly.

    Tip: Elbow stays touching the towel.

  2. Band internal rotation3 × 15

    Strength · Rotator cuff
    1. Elbow at your side, band anchored to the outside.
    2. Rotate the forearm in towards the belly.
    3. Return slowly.

    Tip: Smooth and controlled.

  3. Band pull-apart3 × 15

    Strength · Rear shoulder, upper back
    1. Hold a band at shoulder height, arms straight.
    2. Pull the band apart to touch the chest.
    3. Return slowly.

    Tip: Ribs down, don't arch the back.

  4. Wall push-up plus3 × 10

    Strength · Chest, serratus anterior
    1. Hands on a wall at shoulder height, body straight.
    2. Bend the elbows to bring the chest to the wall.
    3. Push back, then push a little further to spread the shoulder blades.

    Tip: The 'plus' at the end protects the shoulder.

  5. Side plank3 × 20 s

    Core · Obliques, side of the trunk
    1. On your side, elbow under the shoulder.
    2. Knees bent first, then straight legs.
    3. Lift hips into a line and hold.

    Tip: Head, hips, feet in one line.

  6. Side-lying external rotation3 × 12

    Strength · Rotator cuff (infraspinatus)
    1. Lie on your side, towel under the top elbow, elbow bent 90°.
    2. Rotate the forearm up towards the ceiling.
    3. Lower slowly over 3 s.

    Tip: One of the best-studied rotator cuff exercises.

  7. Prone T & Y raise3 × 10

    Strength · Lower & middle trapezius
    1. Lie face down on a bench or the floor.
    2. Lift the arms out to a 'T', then up to a 'Y', thumbs up.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Small lift, no shrugging.

  8. Bent-over row3 × 12

    Strength · Upper back, shoulder blades
    1. Hinge forward with a flat back, weights or band in hands.
    2. Pull the elbows back, squeezing the shoulder blades.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Keep the neck long and the back flat.

Move on when:

  • Band rotations pain-free
  • Full range overhead
Stage 3 · Weeks 6+

Overhead & sport

Goal: Strength overhead and in sport positions.

  1. Dumbbell shoulder press3 × 10

    Strength · Overhead strength
    1. Sit or stand with light dumbbells at shoulder height.
    2. Press up in a pain-free arc.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Start light; pain should stay 3/10 or lower.

  2. Push-up3 × 10

    Strength · Chest, arms, core
    1. Hands under shoulders, body in a straight line (knees down to make it easier).
    2. Lower the chest towards the floor.
    3. Push back up without the hips sagging.

    Tip: Too hard? Start with wall push-ups.

  3. Band external rotation3 × 15

    Strength · Rotator cuff · arm at 90°
    1. Elbow at your side, towel under it, band in hand.
    2. Rotate the forearm outward.
    3. Return slowly.

    Tip: Elbow stays touching the towel.

  4. Band pull-apart3 × 15

    Strength · Rear shoulder, upper back
    1. Hold a band at shoulder height, arms straight.
    2. Pull the band apart to touch the chest.
    3. Return slowly.

    Tip: Ribs down, don't arch the back.

Move on when:

  • Pressing and throwing pain-free

Important: Keep pain during exercise at 3/10 or less. Strength takes 8–12 weeks to change.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Strong

  • Keyhole 'decompression' surgery was no better than placebo surgery for this kind of shoulder pain.
  • Exercise-based rehab is therefore the first-line treatment.

Sources: Beard et al. 2018, The Lancet (CSAW placebo-controlled trial)

Common questions

How long does rotator cuff take to heal?

It depends on severity. Mild: about 30 days (about 4 weeks). Moderate: about 60 days (about 9 weeks). Severe: about 90 days (about 3 months). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Cannot lift the arm at all after a fall (possible tear or dislocation); Pain with fever or redness; Pain spreading down the arm with numbness.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
