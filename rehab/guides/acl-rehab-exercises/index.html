ACL tear / reconstruction: rehab exercises & recovery plan

The anterior cruciate ligament stops the shin sliding forward and twisting. It usually tears on a sudden stop, cut or awkward landing. Many athletes have reconstruction surgery; some recover without it.

Common in: Football, basketball, volleyball · Body area: Knee · Updated November 2026

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

The ACL (anterior cruciate ligament) is a strong band in the centre of the knee. It stops the shin bone sliding forward and twisting under the thigh bone.

How it happens

Most ACL tears happen without contact: landing from a jump, stopping suddenly or cutting with the knee caving inward. Many people feel or hear a 'pop' and the knee swells within a few hours.

How serious can it be?

Grade 1 — stretched

Fibres stretched but the knee is stable.

Grade 2 — partial tear

Some fibres torn; the knee may feel unsteady.

Grade 3 — complete tear

The most common type. The ligament is torn through and the knee can give way when turning.

How it heals

A completely torn ACL does not grow back together by itself. Some people return to sport with rehab alone; many athletes choose reconstruction surgery. Either way, rehab takes 9 months or more — returning to cutting sports earlier greatly raises the risk of another tear.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • HamstringsAct like a 'second ACL' — they pull the shin bone back and stop it sliding forward.
  • QuadricepsNeed to be rebuilt to equal the other leg; weak quads are the biggest re-injury risk.
  • GlutesStop the knee from caving inward when landing and cutting.
  • Gluteus medius (side hip)Side-hip strength keeps the knee lined up over the foot.
  • CalvesHelp absorb landings and protect the knee.
  • Core (abs & obliques)Keeps your body balanced over the knee when you change direction.

Come back stronger

  • Test before you return: both legs should hop and lift almost equally (90%+).
  • Train landing and cutting technique — knees over toes, soft bend.
  • Keep a warm-up like FIFA 11+ forever; it roughly halves ACL injuries.

Typical signs

  • Pop at the moment of injury
  • Fast swelling within hours
  • Knee gives way when turning

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Knee locked and cannot straighten
  • Calf pain, heat and swelling after surgery (possible clot)
  • Fever or wound redness after surgery

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Protect & straighten

Goal: Full knee straightening, wake up the quads, reduce swelling.

  1. Ankle pumps3 × 20

    Mobility · Circulation, ankle range
    1. Lie or sit with the leg straight.
    2. Pull toes up towards you, then point them away.
    3. Keep a steady rhythm.

    Tip: Moves swelling out after injury or surgery.

  2. Quad sets3 × 10

    Isometric · Quadriceps activation · 5 s hold
    1. Sit or lie with the leg straight and a rolled towel under the knee.
    2. Tighten the thigh to press the back of the knee into the towel.
    3. Hold, then relax fully.

    Tip: Watch the kneecap glide up as the muscle switches on.

  3. Straight leg raise3 × 10

    Strength · Quadriceps, hip flexors
    1. Lie on your back, other knee bent.
    2. Lock the straight knee with a quad set first.
    3. Lift the leg to the height of the other knee, lower slowly.

    Tip: If the knee bends during the lift (a 'lag'), go back to quad sets.

  4. Heel slides3 × 15

    Mobility · Knee bending range
    1. Lie on your back.
    2. Slide the heel towards your bottom as far as comfortable.
    3. Slide back to straight.

    Tip: Use a towel around the foot to help if needed.

  5. Prone knee hang3 × 1 min

    Stretch · Knee straightening range
    1. Lie face down with the kneecap just off the end of the bed.
    2. Let gravity straighten the knee.
    3. Relax the thigh completely.

    Tip: Full straightening early is one of the most important goals after ACL surgery.

  6. Kneecap mobilisation2 × 1 min

    Mobility · Kneecap mobility · if cleared
    1. Sit with the leg straight and thigh relaxed.
    2. Gently glide the kneecap up, down, in and out with your fingers.
    3. Hold each direction a few seconds.

    Tip: Only after surgery when your surgeon or physio has cleared it.

Move on when:

  • Knee straightens fully (same as other side)
  • Straight leg raise with no knee bend
  • Walking without crutches as cleared
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–6

Walk normally

Goal: Normal gait, bend to 120°+, basic strength and balance.

  1. Stationary bike10 min

    Cardio · Knee range, fitness
    1. Seat high enough that the knee is almost straight at the bottom.
    2. Low resistance.
    3. Steady pedalling.

    Tip: Rock back and forth first if a full turn is not yet possible.

  2. Mini squat (0–45°)3 × 12

    Strength · Quads, glutes
    1. Feet hip-width, hold a support if needed.
    2. Sit back a little as if to a high chair, knees to about 45°.
    3. Push through both heels to stand.

    Tip: Knees track over the 2nd toe, not caving in.

  3. Terminal knee extension3 × 15

    Strength · Vastus medialis, last degrees of extension
    1. Band around the back of the knee, anchored in front.
    2. Start with the knee slightly bent.
    3. Push the knee back to fully straight against the band, squeeze 2 s.

    Tip: Slow and controlled, no snapping into lock.

  4. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

  5. Step-ups3 × 10

    Strength · Quads, glutes · low step
    1. Face a low step (10–20 cm).
    2. Step up with the working leg and stand tall.
    3. Step down slowly with the other leg.

    Tip: Raise the step height as strength improves.

  6. Side-lying hip abduction3 × 12

    Strength · Glute medius
    1. Lie on your side, bottom knee bent.
    2. Lift the straight top leg slightly behind the body.
    3. Lower slowly, toes pointing forward.

    Tip: Strong hip abductors reduce knee 'caving in'.

  7. Calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves
    1. Stand on both feet, hold a support.
    2. Rise high onto the balls of the feet.
    3. Lower slowly over 3 s.

    Tip: Move to the edge of a step for more range.

  8. Single-leg balance3 × 30 s

    Balance · Balance, ankle reflexes
    1. Stand on one leg, knee soft.
    2. Progress: eyes closed → on a pillow → catching a ball.
    3. Stay tall.

    Tip: Balance training reduces repeat ankle sprains.

  9. Seated knee extension3 × 12

    Strength · Quadriceps · 90°–45° range, if cleared
    1. Sit tall on a chair.
    2. Straighten one knee fully and squeeze the thigh for 2 s.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Add an ankle weight to progress. After ACL surgery, ask your surgeon when this is allowed.

Move on when:

  • Bend 120° or more
  • Walking with no limp
  • Single-leg stand 30 s
  • No swelling after exercise
Stage 3 · Weeks 6–12

Build strength

Goal: Single-leg strength and control.

  1. Squat3 × 10

    Strength · Quads, glutes
    1. Feet shoulder-width (hold a weight at the chest to progress).
    2. Sit down between the heels to a pain-free depth.
    3. Stand tall, squeezing glutes.

    Tip: 3 s down, 3 s up builds strength in tendons too.

  2. Split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes, balance
    1. Long stride stance, back heel up.
    2. Lower the back knee straight down towards the floor.
    3. Drive up through the front heel.

    Tip: Front knee may go over the toes; keep it in line with the foot.

  3. Forward step-down3 × 10

    Strength · Eccentric quad control
    1. Stand on a step on the working leg.
    2. Slowly lower the other heel to touch the floor in front.
    3. Push back up without locking the knee hard.

    Tip: Knee stays in line with the foot. Use a mirror.

  4. Single-leg Romanian deadlift3 × 8

    Strength · Hamstrings, glutes, balance
    1. Stand on one leg with a soft knee.
    2. Hinge forward, the free leg going back, back flat.
    3. Return to standing by squeezing the glute.

    Tip: Hold a wall at first. Hips stay square to the floor.

  5. Single-leg bridge3 × 10

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Bridge position, lift one foot off the floor.
    2. Raise hips level, not twisting.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Hips stay level like a table.

  6. Single-leg calf raise3 × 12

    Strength · Calves, Achilles
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

  7. Y-balance reach3 × 5

    Balance · Dynamic balance · each direction
    1. Stand on one leg.
    2. Reach the other foot forward, back-left, back-right as far as you can.
    3. Touch lightly, return without falling.

    Tip: Measure reach distance; compare sides.

  8. Wall sit3 × 45 s

    Isometric · Quadriceps isometric
    1. Back against a wall, slide down to a pain-free knee angle.
    2. Keep weight in the heels.
    3. Hold the position, breathe normally.

    Tip: Isometric holds often reduce knee and tendon pain for a while afterwards.

  9. Band side walk3 × 10

    Strength · Glute medius
    1. Band around the knees or ankles, knees slightly bent.
    2. Step sideways, keeping tension on the band.
    3. 10 steps one way, then back.

    Tip: Toes point forward; don't let the knees cave in.

  10. Single-leg squat to box3 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes, knee control
    1. Stand on one leg in front of a box or chair.
    2. Slowly sit back until you lightly touch the box.
    3. Stand up on the same leg, knee in line with the 2nd toe.

    Tip: Use a higher box to make it easier.

Move on when:

  • 10 controlled single-leg squats, knee in line
  • Quad strength at least 70% of the other leg
  • No swelling the day after training
Stage 4 · Months 3–6

Run & land

Goal: Start running, jumping and landing safely.

  1. Walk–jog intervals20 min

    Cardio · Return to running · walk–jog
    1. Start: 1 min jog / 1 min walk × 10.
    2. Each pain-free session, add jog time.
    3. Target 20–30 min continuous easy running.

    Tip: Run on flat, even ground first.

  2. Pogo hops3 × 20

    Plyometric · Ankle stiffness, tendon spring
    1. Small quick hops on both feet, knees nearly straight.
    2. Stay on the balls of the feet.
    3. Land quietly.

    Tip: Progress to single-leg pogos.

  3. Drop landing3 × 6

    Plyometric · Landing control
    1. Step off a low box (20–30 cm).
    2. Land on both feet softly, hips back, knees over toes.
    3. Hold the landing 2 s.

    Tip: Quiet landings mean good shock absorption.

  4. Single-leg hop & stick3 × 5

    Plyometric · Power, landing on one leg
    1. Hop forward on one leg.
    2. Land softly and hold for 3 s.
    3. Progress distance, then add lateral and diagonal hops.

    Tip: Compare distance with the uninjured leg — target 90%+.

  5. Lateral bounds3 × 6

    Plyometric · Side-to-side power & control
    1. Jump sideways from one leg to the other.
    2. Land and stick for 2 s before the next.
    3. Increase distance gradually.

    Tip: Knee must not cave inward on landing.

  6. Rear-foot elevated split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Single-leg strength
    1. Back foot on a bench behind you.
    2. Lower down on the front leg under control.
    3. Drive up through the front foot.

    Tip: Add dumbbells or a backpack when 3×10 is easy.

  7. Nordic hamstring curl3 × 5

    Strength · Eccentric hamstrings
    1. Kneel with a partner holding your ankles.
    2. Lower your body forward as slowly as possible, hips straight.
    3. Catch yourself with your hands, push back up.

    Tip: One of the best-proven hamstring injury preventers in football.

  8. Box jump3 × 5

    Plyometric · Power, landing
    1. Stand in front of a low box.
    2. Swing the arms and jump onto the box, landing softly.
    3. Step (don't jump) back down.

    Tip: Land in a quiet half-squat, knees out.

  9. Skipping (A-skips)3 × 20

    Cardio · Running coordination, calf spring
    1. Skip forward driving one knee up.
    2. Land on the ball of the foot.
    3. Swing the arms opposite to the legs.

    Tip: Great warm-up before running and sprinting.

Move on when:

  • 20 min easy running without pain or swelling
  • Hop tests at least 90% of the other leg
  • Quad strength at least 90%
Stage 5 · Months 6–9+

Return to sport

Goal: Cutting, reaction and full team training.

  1. Agility & cutting drills4 × 20 s

    Plyometric · Sport-specific movement
    1. Ladder drills, then cone shuttles.
    2. Planned cuts at 45°, later 90°.
    3. Finally unplanned cuts reacting to a partner's signal.

    Tip: Unplanned cutting is the last step before full training.

  2. Single-leg hop & stick3 × 6

    Plyometric · Power, landing on one leg · add diagonal
    1. Hop forward on one leg.
    2. Land softly and hold for 3 s.
    3. Progress distance, then add lateral and diagonal hops.

    Tip: Compare distance with the uninjured leg — target 90%+.

  3. Copenhagen plank3 × 20 s

    Strength · Adductors (groin)
    1. Side plank with the top leg on a bench.
    2. Short lever: knee on the bench. Long lever: ankle on the bench.
    3. Lift hips and hold, bottom leg free.

    Tip: Shown to cut groin injuries in football by around 40%.

  4. Nordic hamstring curl3 × 6

    Strength · Eccentric hamstrings
    1. Kneel with a partner holding your ankles.
    2. Lower your body forward as slowly as possible, hips straight.
    3. Catch yourself with your hands, push back up.

    Tip: One of the best-proven hamstring injury preventers in football.

  5. Rear-foot elevated split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Single-leg strength · loaded
    1. Back foot on a bench behind you.
    2. Lower down on the front leg under control.
    3. Drive up through the front foot.

    Tip: Add dumbbells or a backpack when 3×10 is easy.

Move on when:

  • At least 9 months after surgery
  • Strength & hop tests 90%+ both legs
  • Full training without problems
  • Confident in the knee — and cleared by surgeon/physio

Important: Follow your surgeon's protocol first. Graft type and extra repairs (meniscus) change the timeline. Most guidelines advise at least 9 months before return to pivoting sport.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Strong

  • Waiting until at least 9 months after surgery before returning to pivoting sport: each month of delay up to 9 months cut the re-injury rate by about half, and more equal quadriceps strength lowered risk further.
  • Neuromuscular warm-up programmes (balance, landing and strength drills, like FIFA 11+) roughly halve the risk of ACL injury in athletes.

Sources: Grindem et al. 2016, British Journal of Sports Medicine (Delaware-Oslo ACL cohort); Gagnier et al. 2013, American Journal of Sports Medicine (meta-analysis); Donnell-Fink et al. 2015, PLoS One (meta-analysis)

Common questions

How long does acl tear take to heal?

It depends on severity. Sprain / partial tear: about 60 days (about 9 weeks). Complete tear, no surgery: about 120 days (about 4 months). After reconstruction: about 270 days (about 9 months). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Knee locked and cannot straighten; Calf pain, heat and swelling after surgery (possible clot); Fever or wound redness after surgery.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
