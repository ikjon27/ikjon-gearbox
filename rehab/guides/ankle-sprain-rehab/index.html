Ankle sprain: rehab exercises & recovery plan

The most common sports injury. The foot rolls inward and stretches or tears the outer ankle ligaments. Proper rehab is key — without it, up to 1 in 3 people sprain it again.

Common in: Football, basketball, running · Body area: Ankle · Updated November 2026

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Start the Ankle sprain plan

Typical signs

  • Swelling and bruising on the outer ankle
  • Pain putting weight on it

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Cannot take 4 steps at all
  • Tender on the bony knobs of the ankle or the outer foot bone (possible fracture — get an X-ray)
  • Numbness or a cold, pale foot

How long does recovery take?

It depends on how serious the injury is. These are typical plan lengths:

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Days 0–7

Early movement

Goal: Control swelling, keep moving, weight-bear as able.

  1. Ankle pumps3 × 20

    Mobility · Circulation, ankle range
    1. Lie or sit with the leg straight.
    2. Pull toes up towards you, then point them away.
    3. Keep a steady rhythm.

    Tip: Moves swelling out after injury or surgery.

  2. Ankle alphabet2 × 1

    Mobility · Ankle range of motion · full alphabet
    1. Sit with the foot off the floor.
    2. Draw the alphabet with your big toe.
    3. Move from the ankle, not the knee.

    Tip: Gentle early movement speeds recovery.

  3. Calf stretch3 × 30 s

    Stretch · Calf & Achilles flexibility · gentle
    1. Hands on a wall, injured leg behind.
    2. Back heel down, knee straight.
    3. Then bend the back knee to stretch the soleus.

    Tip: Hold without bouncing.

  4. Short-foot exercise3 × 10

    Isometric · Arch muscles
    1. Sit or stand with the foot flat.
    2. Pull the ball of the foot towards the heel to raise the arch, toes relaxed.
    3. Hold, then relax.

    Tip: Toes must not curl.

Move on when:

  • Walking with little limp
  • Swelling going down
Stage 2 · Weeks 1–3

Strength & balance

Goal: Restore strength in all directions.

  1. Band ankle 4-way3 × 15

    Strength · Ankle muscles all directions · each direction
    1. Sit with a band around the forefoot.
    2. Push down, pull up, turn in, turn out against the band.
    3. Slow, controlled reps each way.

    Tip: Eversion (turning out) is the key one after a sprain.

  2. Calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves
    1. Stand on both feet, hold a support.
    2. Rise high onto the balls of the feet.
    3. Lower slowly over 3 s.

    Tip: Move to the edge of a step for more range.

  3. Knee-to-wall3 × 10

    Mobility · Ankle dorsiflexion
    1. Face a wall, toes a few cm away.
    2. Drive the knee to touch the wall, heel down.
    3. Move the foot back as range improves.

    Tip: Normal is about 10 cm toe-to-wall. Compare both sides.

  4. Single-leg balance3 × 30 s

    Balance · Balance, ankle reflexes
    1. Stand on one leg, knee soft.
    2. Progress: eyes closed → on a pillow → catching a ball.
    3. Stay tall.

    Tip: Balance training reduces repeat ankle sprains.

  5. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

Move on when:

  • Single-leg stand 30 s eyes open
  • 20 calf raises
Stage 3 · Weeks 3–6

Dynamic control

Goal: Single-leg strength and reactive balance.

  1. Single-leg calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves, Achilles
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

  2. Y-balance reach3 × 5

    Balance · Dynamic balance
    1. Stand on one leg.
    2. Reach the other foot forward, back-left, back-right as far as you can.
    3. Touch lightly, return without falling.

    Tip: Measure reach distance; compare sides.

  3. Single-leg balance3 × 30 s

    Balance · Balance, ankle reflexes · eyes closed
    1. Stand on one leg, knee soft.
    2. Progress: eyes closed → on a pillow → catching a ball.
    3. Stay tall.

    Tip: Balance training reduces repeat ankle sprains.

  4. Pogo hops3 × 20

    Plyometric · Ankle stiffness, tendon spring
    1. Small quick hops on both feet, knees nearly straight.
    2. Stay on the balls of the feet.
    3. Land quietly.

    Tip: Progress to single-leg pogos.

  5. Lateral bounds3 × 6

    Plyometric · Side-to-side power & control
    1. Jump sideways from one leg to the other.
    2. Land and stick for 2 s before the next.
    3. Increase distance gradually.

    Tip: Knee must not cave inward on landing.

  6. Single-leg lateral hop3 × 8

    Plyometric · Ankle & knee control
    1. Stand on one leg.
    2. Hop sideways over a line and land softly.
    3. Hop back. Stick each landing.

    Tip: Knee stays over the foot.

  7. Toe walking2 × 1

    Strength · Calves, ankle stability · 20 m
    1. Rise high onto the balls of your feet.
    2. Walk forward without letting the heels drop.
    3. Walk 10–20 m.

    Tip: Keep the ankles straight, not rolling out.

Move on when:

  • 25 single-leg calf raises
  • Hops without pain
Stage 4 · Weeks 6+

Return to sport

Goal: Running, cutting, sport drills.

  1. Walk–jog intervals20 min

    Cardio · Return to running
    1. Start: 1 min jog / 1 min walk × 10.
    2. Each pain-free session, add jog time.
    3. Target 20–30 min continuous easy running.

    Tip: Run on flat, even ground first.

  2. Single-leg hop & stick3 × 6

    Plyometric · Power, landing on one leg
    1. Hop forward on one leg.
    2. Land softly and hold for 3 s.
    3. Progress distance, then add lateral and diagonal hops.

    Tip: Compare distance with the uninjured leg — target 90%+.

  3. Agility & cutting drills4 × 20 s

    Plyometric · Sport-specific movement
    1. Ladder drills, then cone shuttles.
    2. Planned cuts at 45°, later 90°.
    3. Finally unplanned cuts reacting to a partner's signal.

    Tip: Unplanned cutting is the last step before full training.

  4. Single-leg balance3 × 30 s

    Balance · Balance, ankle reflexes · catching a ball
    1. Stand on one leg, knee soft.
    2. Progress: eyes closed → on a pillow → catching a ball.
    3. Stay tall.

    Tip: Balance training reduces repeat ankle sprains.

  5. Box jump3 × 5

    Plyometric · Power, landing
    1. Stand in front of a low box.
    2. Swing the arms and jump onto the box, landing softly.
    3. Step (don't jump) back down.

    Tip: Land in a quiet half-squat, knees out.

Move on when:

  • Full training pain-free
  • Keep balance work 3×/week for prevention

Important: Use a brace or tape for sport for 6–12 months after return. Balance work is the single best protection against re-injury.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Strong

  • After an ankle sprain, exercise therapy (balance and strength) lowers the odds of spraining again by about 40%.
  • Wearing a brace or tape during sport lowers the odds of re-injury even more — by about 60%.
  • The Ottawa ankle rules (bone tenderness or not able to take 4 steps) pick up about 98% of ankle and midfoot fractures, so they tell you when an X-ray is needed.

Sources: Doherty et al. 2017, British Journal of Sports Medicine (overview of systematic reviews); Bachmann et al. 2003, BMJ (systematic review of the Ottawa rules)

Common questions

How long does ankle sprain take to heal?

It depends on severity. Mild: about 30 days (about 4 weeks). Moderate: about 60 days (about 9 weeks). Severe: about 90 days (about 3 months). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Cannot take 4 steps at all; Tender on the bony knobs of the ankle or the outer foot bone (possible fracture — get an X-ray); Numbness or a cold, pale foot.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
