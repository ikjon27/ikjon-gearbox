IT band syndrome: rehab exercises & recovery plan

Pain on the outside of the knee, usually starting at the same distance into a run. Linked to training jumps and weak hip muscles.

Common in: Running, cycling · Body area: Knee · Updated November 2026

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

The IT band is a thick strip of tissue running down the outside of the thigh from the hip to just below the knee. In IT band syndrome the tissue where it crosses the outer knee becomes irritated.

How it happens

Repeated knee bending in running or cycling — usually after a jump in distance, lots of downhill running, or weak hip muscles letting the knee drop inward.

How serious can it be?

Mild

Pain only after 5+ km.

Moderate

Pain within the first 1–2 km or on stairs.

Persistent

Pain stops running completely. Seek a physio assessment.

How it heals

It settles when running load is reduced and the hips get stronger. Foam rolling and stretching may feel good but do not fix it on their own.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • Gluteus medius (side hip)Keeps the pelvis level and the knee from dropping inward when you run.
  • GlutesControl hip rotation and share the load.
  • QuadricepsSupport the knee on downhill running.
  • Core (abs & obliques)Keeps your pelvis stable on every stride.

Come back stronger

  • Reduce distance and avoid downhill running until pain-free.
  • Run on alternating sides of a sloped road, or on flat ground.
  • Increase weekly distance by about 10% at a time.

Typical signs

  • Sharp outer-knee pain while running
  • Worse running downhill

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Swelling inside the knee
  • Locking

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Calm & hips

Goal: Settle pain, wake up glutes.

  1. Side-lying hip abduction3 × 15

    Strength · Glute medius
    1. Lie on your side, bottom knee bent.
    2. Lift the straight top leg slightly behind the body.
    3. Lower slowly, toes pointing forward.

    Tip: Strong hip abductors reduce knee 'caving in'.

  2. Clamshell3 × 15

    Strength · Glute medius
    1. Lie on your side, knees bent, heels together.
    2. Open the top knee without rolling the pelvis back.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Add a band above the knees for progression.

  3. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

  4. Side plank3 × 30 s

    Core · Obliques, side of the trunk
    1. On your side, elbow under the shoulder.
    2. Knees bent first, then straight legs.
    3. Lift hips into a line and hold.

    Tip: Head, hips, feet in one line.

  5. Stationary bike15 min

    Cardio · Knee range, fitness
    1. Seat high enough that the knee is almost straight at the bottom.
    2. Low resistance.
    3. Steady pedalling.

    Tip: Rock back and forth first if a full turn is not yet possible.

  6. Band side walk3 × 10

    Strength · Glute medius
    1. Band around the knees or ankles, knees slightly bent.
    2. Step sideways, keeping tension on the band.
    3. 10 steps one way, then back.

    Tip: Toes point forward; don't let the knees cave in.

Move on when:

  • Pain-free walking and stairs
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–5

Single-leg strength

Goal: Hip control on one leg.

  1. Single-leg bridge3 × 10

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Bridge position, lift one foot off the floor.
    2. Raise hips level, not twisting.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Hips stay level like a table.

  2. Forward step-down3 × 10

    Strength · Eccentric quad control
    1. Stand on a step on the working leg.
    2. Slowly lower the other heel to touch the floor in front.
    3. Push back up without locking the knee hard.

    Tip: Knee stays in line with the foot. Use a mirror.

  3. Single-leg Romanian deadlift3 × 8

    Strength · Hamstrings, glutes, balance
    1. Stand on one leg with a soft knee.
    2. Hinge forward, the free leg going back, back flat.
    3. Return to standing by squeezing the glute.

    Tip: Hold a wall at first. Hips stay square to the floor.

  4. Split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes, balance
    1. Long stride stance, back heel up.
    2. Lower the back knee straight down towards the floor.
    3. Drive up through the front heel.

    Tip: Front knee may go over the toes; keep it in line with the foot.

  5. Side plank3 × 40 s

    Core · Obliques, side of the trunk
    1. On your side, elbow under the shoulder.
    2. Knees bent first, then straight legs.
    3. Lift hips into a line and hold.

    Tip: Head, hips, feet in one line.

Move on when:

  • Single-leg squat with level hips
Stage 3 · Week 5+

Return to run

Goal: Graded running.

  1. Walk–jog intervals20 min

    Cardio · Return to running
    1. Start: 1 min jog / 1 min walk × 10.
    2. Each pain-free session, add jog time.
    3. Target 20–30 min continuous easy running.

    Tip: Run on flat, even ground first.

  2. Lateral bounds3 × 6

    Plyometric · Side-to-side power & control
    1. Jump sideways from one leg to the other.
    2. Land and stick for 2 s before the next.
    3. Increase distance gradually.

    Tip: Knee must not cave inward on landing.

  3. Rear-foot elevated split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Single-leg strength
    1. Back foot on a bench behind you.
    2. Lower down on the front leg under control.
    3. Drive up through the front foot.

    Tip: Add dumbbells or a backpack when 3×10 is easy.

Move on when:

  • Usual run distance without outer-knee pain

Important: Reduce running volume and downhill running while you strengthen the hips. Stretching the IT band alone rarely fixes it.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Limited

  • Few good trials exist. Reducing running load (especially downhill) and strengthening the hip abductors is the usual approach.
  • Stretching the IT band alone has little support.

Sources: Clinical consensus; small trials only

Common questions

How long does it band syndrome take to heal?

It depends on severity. Mild: about 30 days (about 4 weeks). Moderate: about 60 days (about 9 weeks). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Swelling inside the knee; Locking.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
