Sciatica (pain down the leg): rehab exercises & recovery plan

Leg pain that travels from the low back or buttock down the leg, often below the knee, sometimes with tingling. It usually comes from an irritated nerve root, for example from a disc bulge. Most people get better without surgery within weeks to months.

Common in: Lifting, desk work, all ages · Body area: Back & core · Updated November 2026

Written by: IKJON teamSources: 2 studies and guidelinesLast checked: 9 November 2026How we write health pages →

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

Sciatica is pain from an irritated nerve root as it leaves the lower spine. Usually a bulging disc presses on or inflames the nerve, and pain travels down the buttock and leg along the nerve.

How it happens

Often after lifting or bending, sometimes for no clear reason. Leg pain is usually worse than back pain, and may come with tingling or numbness.

How serious can it be?

Mild

Leg pain comes and goes; you can work and walk.

Moderate

Constant leg pain; sitting and sleeping are hard.

Severe

Numbness or weakness — get a medical review first.

How it heals

Most people improve within 6–12 weeks; the disc bulge often shrinks by itself. Gentle movement and nerve-glide exercises help. Surgery is only for severe weakness or pain that does not improve.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • Core (abs & obliques)Supports the spine so the nerve is less irritated.
  • GlutesReduce strain on the back during walking and lifting.
  • Lower-back musclesGentle back extension often eases disc-type leg pain.
  • HamstringsGentle mobility helps the nerve glide.

Come back stronger

  • Find positions that ease leg pain and use them often.
  • Walk regularly — short and frequent beats long and rare.
  • Go to hospital now for numbness around the groin or new bladder/bowel problems.

Typical signs

  • Leg pain, often worse than the back pain
  • Pain, tingling or numbness below the knee
  • Worse with sitting, coughing or sneezing

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Numbness around the groin or buttocks, or new bladder/bowel problems — go to hospital NOW
  • Foot or leg getting weaker (foot drop)
  • Pain in both legs
  • Fever or unexplained weight loss

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Calm the nerve

Goal: Reduce leg pain and keep moving.

  1. Brisk walk10 min

    Cardio · Fitness, tissue health · several times a day
    1. Walk on flat ground at a pace that raises breathing.
    2. Keep posture tall and arms swinging.

    Tip: For low back pain, walking is one of the best treatments.

  2. Prone press-up2 × 10

    Mobility · Back extension mobility · if it eases leg pain
    1. Lie face down, hands under the shoulders.
    2. Push the chest up, keeping the hips on the floor.
    3. Relax down.

    Tip: Helpful when bending forward hurts; stop if leg pain increases.

  3. Sciatic nerve glide2 × 10

    Mobility · Sciatic nerve mobility
    1. Sit tall on a chair.
    2. Straighten the knee while lifting the head to look up.
    3. Bend the knee while dropping the chin. Move slowly in rhythm.

    Tip: Gentle movement only — a mild pull is fine, it should not increase leg pain afterwards.

  4. Dead bug2 × 6

    Core · Deep core control
    1. On your back, arms up, hips and knees at 90°.
    2. Lower opposite arm and leg slowly.
    3. Keep the low back gently against the floor.

    Tip: Breathe out as the limbs lower.

  5. Glute bridge2 × 10

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

Move on when:

  • Leg pain reducing or moving up towards the back
  • Walking 20 min
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–6

Core control

Goal: Support the spine with endurance.

  1. Bird dog3 × 8

    Core · Back extensors, core · 5 s holds
    1. On hands and knees.
    2. Reach opposite arm and leg out long.
    3. Hold 5–10 s, don't rotate the hips.

    Tip: Imagine a cup of tea on your lower back.

  2. Side plank3 × 20 s

    Core · Obliques, side of the trunk
    1. On your side, elbow under the shoulder.
    2. Knees bent first, then straight legs.
    3. Lift hips into a line and hold.

    Tip: Head, hips, feet in one line.

  3. McGill curl-up3 × 6

    Core · Abdominals with spine protection
    1. On your back, one knee bent, hands under the low back.
    2. Lift head and shoulders just off the floor, neck still.
    3. Hold 8–10 s.

    Tip: The low back stays in its natural curve.

  4. Hip hinge drill3 × 10

    Mobility · Movement pattern
    1. Hold a stick along your spine touching head, upper back and tailbone.
    2. Push the hips back, keeping all 3 contact points.
    3. Return by driving hips forward.

    Tip: The safe way to bend and lift.

  5. Bridge march3 × 10

    Core · Glutes, core
    1. Lift into a bridge.
    2. Lift one foot a little off the floor, then the other.
    3. Keep the hips level the whole time.

    Tip: If the hips drop, go back to normal bridges.

  6. Brisk walk20 min

    Cardio · Fitness, tissue health
    1. Walk on flat ground at a pace that raises breathing.
    2. Keep posture tall and arms swinging.

    Tip: For low back pain, walking is one of the best treatments.

Move on when:

  • Sitting 30 min without leg pain
Stage 3 · Week 6+

Strength & lifting

Goal: Return to lifting, work and sport.

  1. Kettlebell deadlift3 × 8

    Strength · Hips, back strength
    1. Weight between the feet.
    2. Hinge at the hips, flat back, grab the handle.
    3. Stand by driving hips forward.

    Tip: Gradual loading builds a stronger, less sensitive back.

  2. Squat3 × 10

    Strength · Quads, glutes
    1. Feet shoulder-width (hold a weight at the chest to progress).
    2. Sit down between the heels to a pain-free depth.
    3. Stand tall, squeezing glutes.

    Tip: 3 s down, 3 s up builds strength in tendons too.

  3. Split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes, balance
    1. Long stride stance, back heel up.
    2. Lower the back knee straight down towards the floor.
    3. Drive up through the front heel.

    Tip: Front knee may go over the toes; keep it in line with the foot.

  4. Suitcase carry3 × 30 s

    Core · Core anti-side-bend
    1. Hold a heavy weight in one hand.
    2. Walk tall for 20–30 m without leaning.
    3. Switch hands.

    Tip: Shoulders level the whole time.

  5. Pallof press3 × 10

    Core · Core anti-rotation
    1. Stand side-on to a band anchored at chest height.
    2. Hold the band at your chest, then press it straight out.
    3. Hold 2 s without twisting, bring it back.

    Tip: The trunk must not rotate towards the anchor.

Move on when:

  • Normal daily life and training
  • Leg pain gone or minimal

Important: Keep moving within comfort and walk daily. If one position (like the prone press-up) makes the leg pain move up towards the back, that's a good sign — do more of it.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Strong

  • In a large trial, early surgery for sciatica from a disc gave faster leg-pain relief, but after 1 year results were the same as with non-surgical care — about 95% had recovered in both groups.
  • Staying active and exercise are recommended first; surgery is an option if severe pain persists or weakness develops.

Sources: Peul et al. 2007, New England Journal of Medicine (RCT, 283 patients); Foster et al. 2018, The Lancet (Low Back Pain Series)

Common questions

How long does sciatica last?

Many people improve a lot within 6–12 weeks. Staying active and doing nerve-friendly exercises helps. Pain that is getting worse or comes with weakness needs a doctor.

Should I stretch my hamstrings for sciatica?

Hard hamstring stretches can irritate an angry nerve. Gentle nerve-glide movements and walking are usually better early on.

When is sciatica an emergency?

Get urgent help for numbness around the groin or buttocks, new bladder or bowel problems, or leg weakness that is getting worse.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Numbness around the groin or buttocks, or new bladder/bowel problems — go to hospital NOW; Foot or leg getting weaker (foot drop); Pain in both legs; Fever or unexplained weight loss.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
