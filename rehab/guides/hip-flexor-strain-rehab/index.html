Hip flexor (iliopsoas) strain: rehab exercises & recovery plan

Strain of the deep hip flexor at the front of the hip and groin. It hurts to lift the knee, sprint or kick.

Common in: Football, sprinting, martial arts · Body area: Hip & groin · Updated November 2026

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

Groin pain has 5 main types. Doctors group it by where it comes from (Doha agreement). Pick the one that matches where it hurts:

Adductor-relatedIliopsoas-relatedInguinal-relatedPubic-relatedHip-related

What's injured

The iliopsoas is the deep hip flexor. It runs from the lower spine and inside of the pelvis, over the front of the hip, to the top of the thigh bone. It lifts your knee.

How it happens

Sprinting, kicking, or suddenly lifting the knee against resistance — common in football, running and martial arts.

How serious can it be?

Grade 1

Sore at the front of the hip when lifting the knee. 1–3 weeks.

Grade 2

Painful to sprint or kick, weak knee lift. 3–6 weeks.

Severe / long-standing

Big tear or months of pain. See a sports doctor.

How it heals

The muscle heals in a few weeks when loaded gradually. Pain stretching the hip flexor or lifting the knee against your hand points to this type of groin pain.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • Hip flexorsRebuild knee-lift strength slowly, then speed.
  • Core (abs & obliques)Stabilises the lower spine where the iliopsoas attaches.
  • GlutesStrong glutes balance the front of the hip.
  • Adductors (inner thigh)Work closely with the hip flexors in kicking.

Come back stronger

  • Add speed last: slow strength first, then skips, then sprints.
  • Warm up the hip flexors before kicking or sprinting.
  • Avoid long sitting without breaks; move every 45 minutes.

Typical signs

  • Pain at the front of the hip or high groin
  • Pain lifting the knee against resistance (stairs, sprinting)
  • Pain when the hip flexor is stretched

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Sudden severe pain with a pop and you cannot lift the leg (possible avulsion — common in teenagers)
  • Fever, or pain that is worse at night
  • Lump in the groin

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Week 0–1

Calm & activate

Goal: Walk and climb stairs without pain; gentle activation.

  1. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

  2. Dead bug3 × 8

    Core · Deep core control
    1. On your back, arms up, hips and knees at 90°.
    2. Lower opposite arm and leg slowly.
    3. Keep the low back gently against the floor.

    Tip: Breathe out as the limbs lower.

  3. Hip flexor stretch3 × 20 s

    Stretch · Hip front flexibility · gentle — no sharp pain
    1. Half-kneel, back knee on a cushion.
    2. Tuck the pelvis under and squeeze the back glute.
    3. Shift forward gently.

    Tip: Breathe slowly; no bouncing.

  4. Stationary bike10 min

    Cardio · Knee range, fitness
    1. Seat high enough that the knee is almost straight at the bottom.
    2. Low resistance.
    3. Steady pedalling.

    Tip: Rock back and forth first if a full turn is not yet possible.

Move on when:

  • Walking and stairs pain-free
  • Lifting the knee to hip height 2/10 pain or less
Stage 2 · Weeks 1–4

Strength

Goal: Rebuild hip-flexor and core strength.

  1. Straight leg raise3 × 12

    Strength · Quadriceps, hip flexors
    1. Lie on your back, other knee bent.
    2. Lock the straight knee with a quad set first.
    3. Lift the leg to the height of the other knee, lower slowly.

    Tip: If the knee bends during the lift (a 'lag'), go back to quad sets.

  2. Marching on the spot3 × 40 s

    Cardio · Warm-up, heart rate · slow, knee to hip height
    1. Stand tall.
    2. Lift one knee to hip height, then the other.
    3. Swing the arms.

    Tip: Great warm-up for any session.

  3. Bridge march3 × 10

    Core · Glutes, core
    1. Lift into a bridge.
    2. Lift one foot a little off the floor, then the other.
    3. Keep the hips level the whole time.

    Tip: If the hips drop, go back to normal bridges.

  4. Split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes, balance
    1. Long stride stance, back heel up.
    2. Lower the back knee straight down towards the floor.
    3. Drive up through the front heel.

    Tip: Front knee may go over the toes; keep it in line with the foot.

  5. Pallof press3 × 10

    Core · Core anti-rotation
    1. Stand side-on to a band anchored at chest height.
    2. Hold the band at your chest, then press it straight out.
    3. Hold 2 s without twisting, bring it back.

    Tip: The trunk must not rotate towards the anchor.

  6. Side plank3 × 25 s

    Core · Obliques, side of the trunk
    1. On your side, elbow under the shoulder.
    2. Knees bent first, then straight legs.
    3. Lift hips into a line and hold.

    Tip: Head, hips, feet in one line.

Move on when:

  • Straight-leg raise 3×15 pain-free
  • Split squat pain-free
Stage 3 · Weeks 4+

Speed & kicking

Goal: Sprinting, kicking and changes of direction.

  1. Mountain climbers3 × 30 s

    Cardio · Core, heart rate
    1. High plank position, hands under shoulders.
    2. Drive one knee towards the chest, then switch.
    3. Keep hips low and steady.

    Tip: Slow it down to make it a core exercise.

  2. Skipping (A-skips)4 × 1

    Cardio · Running coordination, calf spring
    1. Skip forward driving one knee up.
    2. Land on the ball of the foot.
    3. Swing the arms opposite to the legs.

    Tip: Great warm-up before running and sprinting.

  3. Running strides6 × 1

    Cardio · Speed, hamstring loading
    1. Run 60–80 m, building up gradually.
    2. Reach the target speed in the middle third.
    3. Walk back to recover.

    Tip: Progress 60% → 75% → 90% → 100% effort across sessions.

  4. Lateral bounds3 × 8

    Plyometric · Side-to-side power & control
    1. Jump sideways from one leg to the other.
    2. Land and stick for 2 s before the next.
    3. Increase distance gradually.

    Tip: Knee must not cave inward on landing.

  5. Agility & cutting drills4 × 20 s

    Plyometric · Sport-specific movement
    1. Ladder drills, then cone shuttles.
    2. Planned cuts at 45°, later 90°.
    3. Finally unplanned cuts reacting to a partner's signal.

    Tip: Unplanned cutting is the last step before full training.

Move on when:

  • Sprinting and long kicks pain-free

Important: Add speed last. Rebuild slow knee-lift strength first, then skips, then sprints and long kicks.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Moderate

  • Iliopsoas-related groin pain is one of the five agreed types of groin pain in athletes; pain lifting the knee against resistance or stretching the hip flexor points to it.
  • Treatment is based on progressive loading; there are few randomised trials for this specific injury.

Sources: Weir et al. 2015, British Journal of Sports Medicine (Doha agreement on groin pain)

Common questions

How long does hip flexor take to heal?

It depends on severity. Mild: about 21 days (about 3 weeks). Moderate: about 42 days (about 6 weeks). Severe: about 84 days (about 2 months). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Sudden severe pain with a pop and you cannot lift the leg (possible avulsion — common in teenagers); Fever, or pain that is worse at night; Lump in the groin.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
