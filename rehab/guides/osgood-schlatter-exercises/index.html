Osgood-Schlatter (teen knee pain): rehab exercises & recovery plan

Irritation where the kneecap tendon attaches to the growing shin bone. It causes a tender bump just below the kneecap during growth spurts, especially with lots of running and jumping. It is not dangerous and settles when growth finishes.

Common in: Growing footballers aged 10–15 · Body area: Knee · Updated November 2026

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

In growing children, the patellar tendon attaches to a growth area at the top of the shin bone (the tibial tuberosity). In Osgood-Schlatter this growth area is pulled and irritated, making a tender bump below the kneecap.

How it happens

Growth spurts plus lots of running, jumping and kicking. The bones grow fast and the muscles become tight and pull harder on the growth area.

How serious can it be?

Mild

Can play; sore afterwards.

Moderate

Pain limits running and jumping.

Severe

Stairs and kneeling hurt in daily life.

How it heals

It is not dangerous and settles when growth finishes (usually by 14–16). The bump may stay but stops hurting. Managing load — not total rest — keeps young players active.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • QuadricepsGentle strength (and later stretching) reduces the pull on the growth area.
  • GlutesHelp with landing so the knee takes less.
  • HamstringsBalance the front and back of the thigh.
  • CalvesAbsorb running and jumping forces.

Come back stronger

  • Use the pain ladder: keep sport pain at 2/10 or less.
  • Use knee pads for kneeling and goalkeeping.
  • Talk to the coach about fewer jumping and sprint drills during growth spurts.

Typical signs

  • Tender bony bump below the kneecap
  • Pain kneeling, jumping or sprinting
  • Age 10–15 and growing fast

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Pain at night or at rest
  • Fever or a red, hot knee
  • Whole knee swollen or cannot straighten

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–3

Calm it down

Goal: Reduce load and pain with holds.

  1. Wall sit5 × 30 s

    Isometric · Quadriceps isometric
    1. Back against a wall, slide down to a pain-free knee angle.
    2. Keep weight in the heels.
    3. Hold the position, breathe normally.

    Tip: Isometric holds often reduce knee and tendon pain for a while afterwards.

  2. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

  3. Side-lying hip abduction3 × 12

    Strength · Glute medius
    1. Lie on your side, bottom knee bent.
    2. Lift the straight top leg slightly behind the body.
    3. Lower slowly, toes pointing forward.

    Tip: Strong hip abductors reduce knee 'caving in'.

  4. Calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves
    1. Stand on both feet, hold a support.
    2. Rise high onto the balls of the feet.
    3. Lower slowly over 3 s.

    Tip: Move to the edge of a step for more range.

  5. Single-leg balance3 × 30 s

    Balance · Balance, ankle reflexes
    1. Stand on one leg, knee soft.
    2. Progress: eyes closed → on a pillow → catching a ball.
    3. Stay tall.

    Tip: Balance training reduces repeat ankle sprains.

  6. Side-lying quad stretch2 × 30 s

    Stretch · Front of thigh
    1. Lie on your side.
    2. Bend the top knee and hold the ankle.
    3. Pull the heel towards your bottom, keeping the knees together.

    Tip: Push the hip slightly forward to feel more stretch.

Move on when:

  • Daily activities pain 2/10 or less
Stage 2 · Weeks 3–7

Strengthen

Goal: Build thigh and hip strength.

  1. Squat3 × 10

    Strength · Quads, glutes
    1. Feet shoulder-width (hold a weight at the chest to progress).
    2. Sit down between the heels to a pain-free depth.
    3. Stand tall, squeezing glutes.

    Tip: 3 s down, 3 s up builds strength in tendons too.

  2. Step-ups3 × 10

    Strength · Quads, glutes
    1. Face a low step (10–20 cm).
    2. Step up with the working leg and stand tall.
    3. Step down slowly with the other leg.

    Tip: Raise the step height as strength improves.

  3. Split squat3 × 8

    Strength · Quads, glutes, balance
    1. Long stride stance, back heel up.
    2. Lower the back knee straight down towards the floor.
    3. Drive up through the front heel.

    Tip: Front knee may go over the toes; keep it in line with the foot.

  4. Single-leg bridge3 × 10

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Bridge position, lift one foot off the floor.
    2. Raise hips level, not twisting.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Hips stay level like a table.

  5. Seated knee extension3 × 12

    Strength · Quadriceps
    1. Sit tall on a chair.
    2. Straighten one knee fully and squeeze the thigh for 2 s.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Add an ankle weight to progress. After ACL surgery, ask your surgeon when this is allowed.

  6. Side-lying quad stretch2 × 30 s

    Stretch · Front of thigh
    1. Lie on your side.
    2. Bend the top knee and hold the ankle.
    3. Pull the heel towards your bottom, keeping the knees together.

    Tip: Push the hip slightly forward to feel more stretch.

Move on when:

  • Jogging pain 2/10 or less
Stage 3 · Week 7+

Back to sport

Goal: Running, jumping and full training.

  1. Walk–jog intervals15 min

    Cardio · Return to running
    1. Start: 1 min jog / 1 min walk × 10.
    2. Each pain-free session, add jog time.
    3. Target 20–30 min continuous easy running.

    Tip: Run on flat, even ground first.

  2. Skipping (A-skips)3 × 20

    Cardio · Running coordination, calf spring
    1. Skip forward driving one knee up.
    2. Land on the ball of the foot.
    3. Swing the arms opposite to the legs.

    Tip: Great warm-up before running and sprinting.

  3. Pogo hops3 × 15

    Plyometric · Ankle stiffness, tendon spring
    1. Small quick hops on both feet, knees nearly straight.
    2. Stay on the balls of the feet.
    3. Land quietly.

    Tip: Progress to single-leg pogos.

  4. Drop landing3 × 5

    Plyometric · Landing control
    1. Step off a low box (20–30 cm).
    2. Land on both feet softly, hips back, knees over toes.
    3. Hold the landing 2 s.

    Tip: Quiet landings mean good shock absorption.

  5. Box jump3 × 5

    Plyometric · Power, landing
    1. Stand in front of a low box.
    2. Swing the arms and jump onto the box, landing softly.
    3. Step (don't jump) back down.

    Tip: Land in a quiet half-squat, knees out.

Move on when:

  • Full training with pain 2/10 or less

Important: Use a pain-guided activity ladder: reduce jumping and sprinting until pain is 2/10 or less, then add back step by step. Total rest is not needed.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Moderate

  • A 12-week programme of pain-guided activity changes plus knee and hip strengthening led to 80% of young athletes reporting improvement.
  • Return to full sport often takes longer, so patience and gradual loading are key.

Sources: Rathleff et al. 2020, Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine (cohort study)

Common questions

How long does osgood-schlatter take to heal?

It depends on severity. Mild: about 30 days (about 4 weeks). Moderate: about 60 days (about 9 weeks). Severe: about 90 days (about 3 months). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Pain at night or at rest; Fever or a red, hot knee; Whole knee swollen or cannot straighten.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
