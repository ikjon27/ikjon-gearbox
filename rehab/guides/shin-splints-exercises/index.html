Shin splints (MTSS): rehab exercises & recovery plan

Pain along the inner edge of the shin bone from bone and muscle overload, usually after a jump in running volume or hard surfaces.

Common in: Running, football, military training · Body area: Shin & calf · Updated November 2026

Written by: IKJON teamSources: 1 studies and guidelinesLast checked: 9 November 2026How we write health pages →

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome) is overload of the inner edge of the shin bone and the muscles attached to it, usually along the lower half.

How it happens

A sudden jump in running, new hard surfaces, or worn shoes — common in new runners, football pre-season and military training.

How serious can it be?

Mild

Pain after running; eases during the run.

Moderate

Pain throughout the run.

Severe

Pain walking or at rest — rule out a stress fracture first.

How it heals

Bone adapts to load over weeks. Reduce running enough to keep pain low, then build up slowly. One small, very painful spot on the bone can be a stress fracture — see a doctor.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • CalvesSoleus and calf strength absorb running shock before the bone.
  • Shin musclesShin muscles control the foot landing.
  • Foot musclesArch strength reduces strain on the inner shin.
  • GlutesHelp with running form and load.

Come back stronger

  • Increase running by no more than 10% a week.
  • Mix running surfaces and replace worn shoes.
  • Run with slightly shorter, quicker steps.

Typical signs

  • Diffuse pain along the inner shin (over 5 cm)
  • Hurts at the start of runs

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • One small spot of severe bone pain (possible stress fracture)
  • Pain at rest or at night
  • Numbness or tight, swollen calf

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Offload

Goal: Rest from running, strengthen lower leg.

  1. Tibialis raise3 × 20

    Strength · Front shin muscles
    1. Lean back against a wall, heels 30 cm away.
    2. Lift the toes and forefoot towards the shins.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Strengthens the shin muscles that suffer in shin splints.

  2. Calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves
    1. Stand on both feet, hold a support.
    2. Rise high onto the balls of the feet.
    3. Lower slowly over 3 s.

    Tip: Move to the edge of a step for more range.

  3. Bent-knee calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Soleus
    1. Stand with knees bent about 30° (or seated with weight on the knees).
    2. Rise onto the toes keeping the knee bend.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: The soleus takes huge load when running.

  4. Short-foot exercise3 × 10

    Isometric · Arch muscles
    1. Sit or stand with the foot flat.
    2. Pull the ball of the foot towards the heel to raise the arch, toes relaxed.
    3. Hold, then relax.

    Tip: Toes must not curl.

  5. Stationary bike20 min

    Cardio · Knee range, fitness
    1. Seat high enough that the knee is almost straight at the bottom.
    2. Low resistance.
    3. Steady pedalling.

    Tip: Rock back and forth first if a full turn is not yet possible.

Move on when:

  • Walking and hopping 10× without pain
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–5

Strengthen

Goal: Capacity for impact.

  1. Single-leg calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves, Achilles
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

  2. Tibialis raise3 × 20

    Strength · Front shin muscles
    1. Lean back against a wall, heels 30 cm away.
    2. Lift the toes and forefoot towards the shins.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Strengthens the shin muscles that suffer in shin splints.

  3. Single-leg balance3 × 30 s

    Balance · Balance, ankle reflexes
    1. Stand on one leg, knee soft.
    2. Progress: eyes closed → on a pillow → catching a ball.
    3. Stay tall.

    Tip: Balance training reduces repeat ankle sprains.

  4. Glute bridge3 × 12

    Strength · Glutes, hamstrings
    1. Lie on your back, knees bent, feet flat.
    2. Squeeze glutes and lift hips into a straight line.
    3. Lower slowly.

    Tip: Push through heels; don't arch the low back.

  5. Pogo hops3 × 15

    Plyometric · Ankle stiffness, tendon spring · low
    1. Small quick hops on both feet, knees nearly straight.
    2. Stay on the balls of the feet.
    3. Land quietly.

    Tip: Progress to single-leg pogos.

  6. Heel walking3 × 1

    Strength · Front shin muscles · 20 m
    1. Lift the toes and walk on your heels.
    2. Keep the knees straight and stand tall.
    3. Walk 10–20 m.

    Tip: Great for shin splints prevention.

  7. Toe walking3 × 1

    Strength · Calves, ankle stability · 20 m
    1. Rise high onto the balls of your feet.
    2. Walk forward without letting the heels drop.
    3. Walk 10–20 m.

    Tip: Keep the ankles straight, not rolling out.

Move on when:

  • 25 single-leg calf raises
  • 30 single-leg hops pain-free
Stage 3 · Week 5+

Graded running

Goal: Return to running gradually.

  1. Walk–jog intervals20 min

    Cardio · Return to running · soft surface
    1. Start: 1 min jog / 1 min walk × 10.
    2. Each pain-free session, add jog time.
    3. Target 20–30 min continuous easy running.

    Tip: Run on flat, even ground first.

  2. Pogo hops3 × 20

    Plyometric · Ankle stiffness, tendon spring
    1. Small quick hops on both feet, knees nearly straight.
    2. Stay on the balls of the feet.
    3. Land quietly.

    Tip: Progress to single-leg pogos.

  3. Single-leg calf raise3 × 15

    Strength · Calves, Achilles
    1. Stand on one foot, light hand support.
    2. Rise as high as possible.
    3. Lower over 3 s.

    Tip: Healthy target: 25+ reps each side.

Move on when:

  • Run 30 min pain-free on grass then road

Important: Bone needs rest from impact but responds to strength work. Increase running by no more than 10% a week.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Limited

  • No treatment has been strongly proven; the available trials are small and have a high risk of bias.
  • Relative rest from impact, strengthening the lower leg, and a slow graded return to running remain the standard approach.

Sources: Winters et al. 2013, Sports Medicine (systematic review)

Common questions

Can I keep running with shin splints?

Reduce your running until the shin is only mildly sore, and swap some runs for cycling or swimming. If one spot on the bone is very tender, or it hurts at rest or at night, see a doctor to rule out a stress fracture.

What causes shin splints?

Usually a quick increase in running, jumping or hard surfaces before the bone and muscles have adapted. Weak calves, worn shoes and long strides can add to the load.

How long do shin splints take to heal?

Mild cases often settle in a few weeks with less running and calf strengthening. More severe cases can take 2–3 months. Increase your running gradually when you return.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: One small spot of severe bone pain (possible stress fracture); Pain at rest or at night; Numbness or tight, swollen calf.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
