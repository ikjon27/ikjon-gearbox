Tennis elbow: rehab exercises & recovery plan

Overload of the forearm tendon on the outer elbow from gripping and wrist extension — common in racket sports and workers.

Common in: Tennis, padel, manual work · Body area: Elbow & wrist · Updated November 2026

Education, not diagnosis. This guide explains how this injury is usually rehabilitated. It cannot tell you what injury you have — see a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm it, especially after a fall, a pop or surgery.

Understand the injury

What's injured

Tennis elbow is overload of the tendon where the wrist-extensor muscles attach to the bony bump on the outer elbow (lateral epicondyle). These muscles lift the wrist and help you grip.

How it happens

Repeated gripping and wrist work — racket sports, tools, carrying bags, typing or a mouse. Only a few cases come from tennis.

How serious can it be?

Mild

Pain only with heavy gripping.

Moderate

Lifting a cup or kettle hurts.

Long-standing

Pain for over 3 months — still improves with loading.

How it heals

Most cases settle within a year, faster with tendon loading exercises. Steroid injections help short-term but results are worse later — exercise is the better long-term choice.

Muscles to strengthen — recover and come back stronger

  • ForearmsSlow, heavy wrist-extension loading is the main treatment.
  • Rotator cuffA strong shoulder takes strain off the forearm.
  • Upper backBetter arm position for gripping and lifting.

Come back stronger

  • Lift and carry with the palm facing up when you can.
  • Use a thicker grip on tools and rackets.
  • Keep wrist exercises 2–3 times a week after it settles.

Typical signs

  • Pain on the outer elbow when gripping
  • Pain lifting a cup or kettle

See a doctor first if you have any of these

  • Numbness or tingling in the hand
  • Swelling or locking of the elbow
  • Pain after a fall

The rehab plan, stage by stage

Move to the next stage only when you meet its “move on when” checks — not just because the weeks have passed.

Stage 1 · Weeks 0–2

Isometric relief

Goal: Reduce pain.

  1. Isometric wrist extension5 × 45 s

    Isometric · Forearm extensors
    1. Forearm on a table, palm down, hand off the edge.
    2. Push the back of the hand up against your other hand.
    3. Hold at moderate effort.

    Tip: Settles tennis elbow pain.

  2. Grip squeeze3 × 10

    Strength · Grip strength
    1. Squeeze a soft ball or towel.
    2. Hold 3–5 s.
    3. Relax fully.

    Tip: Squeeze with the elbow straight later in rehab.

  3. Forearm extensor stretch3 × 30 s

    Stretch · Extensor flexibility
    1. Arm straight in front, palm down.
    2. Gently bend the wrist down with the other hand.
    3. Hold.

    Tip: Gentle stretch only.

Move on when:

  • Gripping a cup with little pain
Stage 2 · Weeks 2–8

Tendon strength

Goal: Progressive slow loading.

  1. Eccentric wrist extension3 × 15

    Strength · Forearm extensors
    1. Forearm on a table, palm down, light dumbbell.
    2. Use the other hand to lift the wrist up.
    3. Lower slowly over 3–4 s alone.

    Tip: Mild discomfort is OK; sharp pain is not.

  2. Forearm rotation with hammer3 × 10

    Strength · Pronators & supinators
    1. Hold a hammer by the handle, elbow at 90°.
    2. Slowly rotate palm up then palm down.
    3. Hold lower on the handle to make it easier.

    Tip: Slow and controlled.

  3. Grip squeeze3 × 10

    Strength · Grip strength · elbow straight
    1. Squeeze a soft ball or towel.
    2. Hold 3–5 s.
    3. Relax fully.

    Tip: Squeeze with the elbow straight later in rehab.

Move on when:

  • 3×15 eccentrics with 2 kg pain 3/10 or less
Stage 3 · Week 8+

Return

Goal: Heavier grip and sport.

  1. Eccentric wrist extension3 × 12

    Strength · Forearm extensors · heavier
    1. Forearm on a table, palm down, light dumbbell.
    2. Use the other hand to lift the wrist up.
    3. Lower slowly over 3–4 s alone.

    Tip: Mild discomfort is OK; sharp pain is not.

  2. Suitcase carry3 × 30 s

    Core · Core anti-side-bend
    1. Hold a heavy weight in one hand.
    2. Walk tall for 20–30 m without leaning.
    3. Switch hands.

    Tip: Shoulders level the whole time.

  3. Forearm rotation with hammer3 × 12

    Strength · Pronators & supinators
    1. Hold a hammer by the handle, elbow at 90°.
    2. Slowly rotate palm up then palm down.
    3. Hold lower on the handle to make it easier.

    Tip: Slow and controlled.

Move on when:

  • Full play or work without flare-ups

Important: Change grip size or technique if it comes from sport.

What the research says

Strength of evidence: Strong

  • Physiotherapy (mobilisation plus exercise) beat wait-and-see at 6 weeks.
  • Steroid injections worked best at 6 weeks, but 72% of patients relapsed and they did worse than physiotherapy at 1 year.

Sources: Bisset et al. 2006, BMJ (RCT)

Common questions

How long does tennis elbow take to heal?

It depends on severity. Mild: about 60 days (about 9 weeks). Moderate: about 90 days (about 3 months). Long-standing: about 120 days (about 4 months). Move on by meeting each stage's checks, not by the calendar alone.

When should I see a doctor?

See a doctor or physiotherapist to confirm the injury, and get help urgently if you notice: Numbness or tingling in the hand; Swelling or locking of the elbow; Pain after a fall.

Should exercises hurt?

Mild discomfort up to about 3–4 out of 10 that settles within 24 hours is usually acceptable. Stop if pain is sharp, goes above 5 out of 10, or swelling increases afterwards.
